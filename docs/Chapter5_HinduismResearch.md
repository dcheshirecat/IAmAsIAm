# Chapter 5, Hinduism: research for the owner (2026-11-12)

## Hinduism is many traditions
- **The main streams:** Vaishnava (Vishnu and his avatars, Krishna and Rama), Shaiva (Shiva), Shakta (the Goddess: Durga, Kali,
  Lakshmi), Smarta (several deities), and **Vedanta** (philosophy: the self and Brahman).
- **Regional and family practice:** worship looks very different from family to family and region to region.
- **Movements with a strong presence in the US:**
  - **The Ramakrishna Order / Vedanta Societies:** monastic swamis who teach anyone who comes.
  - **BAPS Swaminarayan:** large stone mandirs with visitor centres and tours.
  - **ISKCON (Hare Krishna):** kirtan and an open Sunday feast.
  - **Temple communities:** for example South Indian temples to Venkateswara, Rama or Murugan.
- **A tie to Chicago:** Swami Vivekananda's "Sisters and brothers of America" speech at the 1893 World's Parliament of
  Religions, in Chicago.

## What's open to visitors (shown as Maya's practice)
- **Temple visits:** darshan (seeing the deity), aarti (the lamp offering, with everyone singing), and receiving prasad (blessed
  food).
- **Shared practice:** kirtan and bhajan (singing together), meditation and talks at a Vedanta Society, and yoga as practised in
  the tradition.
- **Festivals open to all:** Diwali (lamps), Holi (colours), Navaratri (garba dancing), Ganesh Chaturthi.
- **Visiting manners:** shoes off, dressing modestly, and offerings given and received with the right hand.

## Not shown as her own (closed, teacher-led or hereditary)
- **The priest's rites:** the temple priest's role (pujari / archaka) needs long training and, in many traditions, is hereditary.
  Maya watches; she doesn't perform puja for others.
- **Initiation and family rites:** initiation (diksha) from a guru, the sacred thread ceremony (upanayana), and life-cycle rites
  for others.
- **Caution:** some gurus and groups are high-control. The game shouldn't show a guru Maya surrenders herself to. That matters for
  a dissociative system (boundaries, consent).

## Options for the guide
1. **A swami at a Vedanta Society** (a monk of the Ramakrishna Order). Calm, scholarly, teaches all comers: the self, which no
   part of you can lose. Strong for a system: "you are not your body, not your mind, not your memories". But be careful: that can
   feel like depersonalization, so the swami makes it gentle.
2. **A temple priest (pujari) at a South Indian temple in the suburbs.** A busy family man; aarti, festivals; a kind, practical
   guide to the temple's rhythm. He'd be from a priestly family (hereditary), trained for years.
3. **A Hindu chaplain** (now a real, growing profession in US hospitals and universities, often women). It mirrors Amal in
   Chapter 4, so maybe too similar.
4. **An "aunty"**: a Hindu mother or grandmother next door or from the museum, who runs her household's daily puja at a home
   shrine, makes Maya part of Diwali and teaches her by doing. The most personal; no formal title.
5. **A kirtan teacher at ISKCON**: music, chanting, the Sunday feast. Joyful; but ISKCON has a missionary side, so the story
   would keep a boundary.
6. **A woman teacher or scholar of Shakta practice (the Goddess)**: Durga Puja and Navaratri. A strong female guide; ties to the
   alters' care and protection (Heather).
- **Claude's suggestion:** combine 4 and 1. An aunty (the guide) brings Maya into her family's festivals and home shrine, and
  takes her to the Vedanta Society's talks, where a swami appears. Show Vaishnava, Shaiva and Shakta practice through festivals
  and temples.

## Women as leaders in Hinduism (owner's question)
Yes, in several ways, though it varies by tradition.
- **Women priests (purohita).** Since the 1970s, institutions in Pune, India (Shankar Seva Samiti, Jnana Prabodhini, the
  Bhandarkar Oriental Research Institute) have trained around 1,600 women to perform household rites: weddings, house blessings,
  pujas. In diaspora communities, women priests are still unusual but growing.
- **Temple priests in big South Indian (Agamic) temples** are still almost always men, often from hereditary priestly families.
  A woman in that role would be rare, and therefore a story in itself.
- **Women monastics:** Sri Sarada Math, the women's order of the Ramakrishna tradition (its nuns are called pravrajikas; the
  Vedanta Society of Southern California has a convent in Santa Barbara). Women ascetics are called sadhvis.
- **Women gurus**, widely revered: Sarada Devi (Ramakrishna's wife, the "Holy Mother"), Anandamayi Ma, and Mata Amritanandamayi
  ("Amma", famous for embracing people).
- **The Goddess traditions (Shakta)** put the divine feminine at the centre; in many homes women lead the daily puja and the
  festivals.
- **Hindu chaplains** in the US are often women (Swami Sarvaananda was the first board-certified Hindu chaplain, in 2001).
- Sources: Women priests of Pune (Google Arts & Culture): https://artsandculture.google.com/asset/women-priests-of-pune/EwG8U3YE1La7Rw ;
  Women's eNews on Pune's women priests: https://womensenews.org/2008/02/indian-city-opens-doorway-female-hindu-priests/ ;
  "Women priests" (academic paper, 2022): https://serialsjournals.com/abstract/43877_8-women_priests_final_december_2022.pdf
