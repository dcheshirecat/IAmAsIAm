# Chapter 3, Buddhism: research for the owner (2026-11-12)

The owner's direction: this is where Maya **first gets real help, a therapist and a diagnosis**. The owner asked for research
on **open / shared Buddhist practices** and a proposal for the guide (a monk at a local temple, or travelling to a Buddhist
country after the diagnosis). Nothing here is canon until the owner decides.

## What's open to visitors and non-Buddhists (shown as Maya's practice)
- **Sitting meditation (zazen, vipassana, samatha)**, at public sittings: Zen temples and Theravada centres in Chicago hold
  weekly sittings open to anyone, Buddhist or not.
- **Walking meditation**, and Plum Village (Thich Nhat Hanh) style mindfulness: tea meditation, mindful eating, bells.
- **Loving-kindness (metta)**, taught to the public in most traditions.
- **Chanting with the community**: the nembutsu ("Namu Amida Butsu") in Pure Land / Jodo Shinshu services, sutra chanting at
  Zen services, Tara or Chenrezig chanting at Tibetan centres' public practices.
- **Bowing, incense, a dana (giving) offering** at the altar, following the temple's lead.
- **Festivals:** Vesak (the Buddha's birth, awakening and passing) and Obon (Bon Odori dancing, open to everyone).

## What's NOT shown as Maya's own practice (closed or teacher-led)
- **Ordination** and monastic life.
- **Vajrayana empowerments and tantric practices** that need initiation from a teacher. Public chanting at a Tibetan centre is
  fine to show; empowerments are only shown from the outside, if at all.
- **Monastic rules**, which the story respects: for example, in Theravada a woman doesn't touch a monk or hand him something
  directly. She places it on a cloth.

## Safety, which matters for an OSDD story
- Meditation can bring on **dissociation, depersonalization and re-experiencing trauma**. Britton and Lindahl's "Varieties of
  Contemplative Experience" study documented 59 kinds of challenging experiences. People who were already in distress are more
  likely to have side effects.
- **Trauma-sensitive mindfulness** (David Treleaven) keeps people inside their window of tolerance. Its tools include eyes open,
  short sits, external anchors (sounds, the feet, an object), walking instead of sitting, and permission to stop.
- **A story beat this suggests:** Maya tries a long silent sit and dissociates. Her therapist, or the guide, explains why. The
  practice is changed for her (eyes open, walking, short, a grounding object), and that's where it starts to help. It fits the
  game's grounding skills (`five-senses`, `feet-on-floor`, `grounding-object`) and DBT mindfulness.

## Buddhist streams Maya could meet in Chicago (real places; fictional people)
- **Jodo Shinshu (Shin, Pure Land)**, Japanese American: the Buddhist Temple of Chicago (Uptown), Midwest Buddhist Temple
  (Old Town). Sunday services in English, a "come as you are" teaching, Obon.
- **Zen:** the Zen Buddhist Temple of Chicago (Soto; Evanston; open zazen and intro workshops), the Chicago Zen Buddhist Temple
  (Buddhist Society for Compassionate Wisdom, Lake View; Korean lineage).
- **Theravada (Thai):** Wat Dhammaram (Southwest Side), and Thai meditation centres.
- **Tibetan (Kagyu):** Chicago Karma Thegsum Chöling, with free weekly public practice.
- **Vietnamese Mahayana / Plum Village style:** to confirm a specific community before naming one.
- **Secular mindfulness** (MBSR, DBT mindfulness), from Maya's therapist: not Buddhism, but rooted in it.

## Guide options for the owner
1. **A minister ("Reverend") at a Jodo Shinshu temple in Chicago.** Shin Buddhism's core is being accepted *as you are*,
   without having to fix yourself first. That fits a newly diagnosed system, and it echoes the title. The services are chanting
   together, not long silent sits.
2. **A Thai Theravada monk at a local wat.** Meditation, the dana food offering, the monastic rules. The meditation risk is
   shown and handled.
3. **Travel after the diagnosis** (for example a temple stay in Japan, or a meditation retreat in Thailand). This is dramatic,
   and the retreat is where things go wrong and get changed. It overlaps with Chapter 13's travel to Europe, and a long silent
   retreat right after a diagnosis is exactly what trauma-sensitive teachers warn against. If chosen, the game should show that
   honestly.
- **Claude's suggestion:** stay in Chicago (her therapist is there), with option 1 as the guide. Show Zen, Theravada, Tibetan
  and Plum Village style practice through sittings, Vesak and Obon. The "meditation makes it worse, then a trauma-sensitive
  version helps" arc goes in the middle.
